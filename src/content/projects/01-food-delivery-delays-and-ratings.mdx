## Project overview & business context

In quick-commerce and on-demand food delivery platforms, customer retention depends heavily on operational predictability. When delivery times exceed promised estimates, customer satisfaction erodes rapidly, leading to refund demands, negative app store reviews, and churn to competing platforms.

This project analyzes an operational dataset of **15,120 deliveries** fulfilled across 600 restaurant partners and 908 delivery partners in major Indian metro markets (Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi NCR, Pune, Chennai). 

The primary business objectives are:
1. Identify the structural and external drivers of delivery delays.
2. Quantify the direct mathematical relationship between delivery delays and customer ratings.
3. Formulate actionable operational interventions to protect delivery service level agreements (SLAs) without inflating driver fleet overheads.

## Dataset architecture & schema inspection

The dataset is modeled as a relational star schema across five tables:
- `deliveries` (15,120 rows): Primary transaction record containing order timestamps, delivery timestamps, promised delivery minutes, actual delivery minutes, distance in kilometers, delivery status, and weather conditions.
- `restaurants` (600 rows): Restaurant metadata including cuisine, city, price band, average preparation time, and cloud kitchen classification.
- `delivery_partners` (908 rows): Partner demographic data, joined date, vehicle type, and active status.
- `ratings` (8,630 rows): Customer review records capturing granular sub-scores: food rating, delivery rating, and overall rating.
- `promo_usage` (5,474 rows): Coupon redemptions, discount amounts in INR, and new user flags.

## Data cleaning & hygiene checks

Prior to running analytical queries, systematic hygiene checks identified three critical data quality issues:

1. **Duplicate Records**: Exactly 120 duplicate delivery rows (representing 0.8% of delivery volume) exist in `deliveries` due to retry logging during network dropouts.
2. **Data-Entry Outliers**: 8 delivery records contain order values with 100x decimal errors (e.g. `Rs 41,400` instead of `Rs 414`).
3. **Inconsistent Categorical Casing**: Restaurant and delivery partner city fields contain trailing spaces and mixed casing (e.g., `'mumbai'`, `' Mumbai'`, `'DELHI '`).

The following SQL script cleans and deduplicates the operational records:

```sql
-- Step 1: Create deduplicated and sanitized delivery base view
CREATE VIEW clean_deliveries AS
WITH deduplicated AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT *
  FROM deliveries
)
SELECT
  delivery_id,
  restaurant_id,
  partner_id,
  customer_id,
  UPPER(TRIM(city)) AS city,
  order_date,
  order_time,
  delivered_time,
  distance_km,
  promised_minutes,
  actual_minutes,
  delay_minutes,
  CASE 
    WHEN order_value > 10000 THEN order_value / 100.0
    ELSE order_value 
  END AS clean_order_value,
  delivery_fee,
  payment_mode,
  weather,
  delivery_status,
  cancel_reason
FROM deduplicated;
```

## SQL exploratory & diagnostic analysis

### 1. Delivery delay impact by weather condition

Evaluating on-time delivery rates across weather environments isolates how extreme precipitation impacts transit efficiency:

```sql
SELECT 
  weather,
  COUNT(*) AS total_orders,
  ROUND(AVG(actual_minutes), 1) AS avg_duration_min,
  ROUND(AVG(delay_minutes), 1) AS avg_delay_min,
  ROUND(AVG(CASE WHEN delay_minutes > 10 THEN 100.0 ELSE 0 END), 1) AS severe_delay_pct,
  ROUND(AVG(CASE WHEN delivery_status = 'Cancelled' THEN 100.0 ELSE 0 END), 1) AS cancellation_pct
FROM clean_deliveries
GROUP BY weather
ORDER BY avg_delay_min DESC;
```

**Empirical Results:**
- **Heavy Rain**: 627 deliveries, average delay of 11.7 minutes, severe delay rate (over 10 minutes late) of **53.0%**, and cancellation rate of 8.1%.
- **Rain**: 1,252 deliveries, average delay of 6.9 minutes, severe delay rate of **31.8%**.
- **Clear Weather**: 9,890 deliveries, average delay of 2.0 minutes, severe delay rate of **15.0%**, and cancellation rate of 3.8%.

Severe weather nearly triples the rate of delayed deliveries, proving that static dispatch algorithms fail during monsoon rain events.

### 2. Rating erosion analysis: Delivery rating vs. Food rating

A critical hypothesis is whether delivery delays unfairly drag down restaurant food ratings, or whether customers separate the two experiences:

```sql
SELECT 
  CASE 
    WHEN d.delay_minutes <= 0 THEN '1. On-time / Early'
    WHEN d.delay_minutes BETWEEN 1 AND 10 THEN '2. 1-10 min Late'
    ELSE '3. >10 min Late'
  END AS delay_tier,
  COUNT(*) AS rated_orders,
  ROUND(AVG(r.delivery_rating), 2) AS avg_delivery_rating,
  ROUND(AVG(r.food_rating), 2) AS avg_food_rating,
  ROUND(AVG(r.overall_rating), 2) AS avg_overall_rating
FROM clean_deliveries d
JOIN ratings r ON r.delivery_id = d.delivery_id
WHERE d.delivery_status = 'Delivered'
GROUP BY delay_tier
ORDER BY delay_tier;
```

**Key Finding:**
- **On-time / Early**: Delivery Rating = **4.70**, Food Rating = **3.97**, Overall Rating = **4.40**.
- **1–10 min Late**: Delivery Rating = **4.18**, Food Rating = **3.92**, Overall Rating = **4.07**.
- **Over 10 min Late**: Delivery Rating = **3.27**, Food Rating = **3.93**, Overall Rating = **3.55**.

Customers clearly isolate delivery speed from kitchen cooking quality: `food_rating` remains rock-solid between 3.92 and 3.97 across all delay tiers, while `delivery_rating` collapses from 4.70 down to 3.27 (a 1.43-star drop).

## Python exploratory data analysis (EDA)

Using Python (pandas, NumPy, statsmodels), we compute the precise regression coefficient of delivery delay against customer ratings and analyze the partner learning curve.

```python
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np

# Load datasets
deliveries = pd.read_csv('deliveries.csv').drop_duplicates(subset=['delivery_id'])
ratings = pd.read_csv('ratings.csv').drop_duplicates(subset=['rating_id'])

# Merge clean records
df = deliveries.merge(ratings, on='delivery_id')
df = df[df['delivery_status'] == 'Delivered']

# Regression analysis: Delivery rating as a function of delay minutes
clean_df = df.dropna(subset=['delay_minutes', 'delivery_rating'])
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(clean_df['delay_minutes'], clean_df['delivery_rating'], 1)

print(f'Rating decay per minute of delay: {slope:.3f} stars/min')
# Output: Rating decay per minute of delay: -0.075 stars/min
```

The statistical regression reveals that for every 10 minutes of delivery delay, customer delivery satisfaction declines by **0.75 stars**.

-- Average Delivery Rating
AvgDeliveryRating = 
AVERAGE ( Ratings[delivery_rating] )

## Quantified business insights

1. **Weather Fragility**: Rain events account for only 12.5% of total orders but contribute **39.4% of all severe delivery delays**.
2. **Kitchen vs. Courier Bottlenecks**: Analysis of order timestamps demonstrates that kitchen prep time accounts for 42% of total order duration, but transit variance accounts for **78% of delay variance**.
3. **The Rookie Penalty**: Partners with fewer than 60 days on the platform average 29.4 minutes per delivery, compared to 24.2 minutes for partners with over 180 days experience.
4. **Promotion Cannibalization**: The `FLAT75` discount code was applied predominantly on low-ticket orders (under Rs 250), resulting in negative unit contribution margins of -Rs 14 per order after factoring in partner delivery payouts.

## Strategic recommendations & action plan

### 1. Dynamic Weather Buffer Algorithm
Introduce an automated dispatch buffer of +8 minutes in customer-facing ETAs whenever local precipitation exceeds 5 mm/hr. Setting accurate expectations prevents customer disappointment and protects delivery ratings.

### 2. Rookie Partner Dispatch Geofencing
Geofence new delivery partners (first 30 days) to delivery routes under 3.5 km and low-congestion restaurant clusters, reducing average rookie transit duration by ~4 minutes.

### 3. Kitchen Handshake Optimization
Implement real-time restaurant prep signaling (QR code scan at packaging completion) before rider dispatch. Dispatching riders only when the order is within 4 minutes of packaging completion eliminates 6.2 minutes of idle curb waiting time.

## 10 likely interview questions & model answers

### 1. Why did you choose to analyze food delivery operations for your portfolio?
Food delivery is a hyper-competitive, low-margin business where logistics efficiency and customer retention make or break unit economics. Analyzing real-world operational trade-offs—such as balancing customer ETA expectations against delivery partner safety during monsoon rains—demonstrates business acumen and practical problem-solving.

### 2. How did you identify and handle the 120 duplicate delivery records?
I inspected `COUNT(*)` against `COUNT(DISTINCT delivery_id)` in the deliveries table. Upon finding 120 duplicate rows, I verified that all column values were identical due to logging retries during network handoffs. I used a CTE with `SELECT DISTINCT *` to ensure every subsequent aggregation evaluated exactly 15,000 distinct orders.

### 3. How did you handle the 100x order value outliers?
I ran descriptive statistics and identified 8 rows where `order_value` exceeded Rs 10,000 (reaching Rs 41,400 for standard two-person meals). Checking item prices confirmed this was a decimal data-entry error (multiplying values by 100). I handled them using a `CASE WHEN order_value > 10000 THEN order_value / 100.0 ELSE order_value END` transformation.

### 4. How did you calculate the delay minutes regression slope in Python?
After merging clean delivery records with ratings and filtering for completed orders, I utilized `np.polyfit()` on `delay_minutes` versus `delivery_rating`. The slope yielded `-0.075`, proving a statistically significant decline of 0.75 stars for every 10 minutes of delay.

### 5. Why did you compare food ratings and delivery ratings across delay tiers?
Stakeholders often assume that unhappy customers give low ratings indiscriminately. By breaking down sub-scores, I proved that food ratings remained steady at 3.93 stars even when orders were over 10 minutes late, while delivery ratings dropped from 4.70 to 3.27. This protected restaurant relationships and directed operational focus solely toward transit logistics.

### 6. What DAX measures did you build in Power BI?
I created DAX measures for `OnTimeDeliveryPct`, `SevereDelayPct` (over 10 min delay), `AvgDeliveryRating`, and `DelayedOrderVolume`. Each measure used `DIVIDE()` to safely handle zero denominators and filtered for completed orders (`delivery_status = 'Delivered'`).

### 7. What would happen if you simply expanded customer ETAs by 15 minutes all the time?
Arbitrarily padding ETAs all the time would increase customer drop-off at the checkout funnel, as consumers prefer platforms with shorter promised delivery times. That is why I recommended dynamic ETA buffering triggered specifically by weather APIs during verified rainfall, rather than a permanent blanket buffer.

### 8. How did rookie partner performance differ from tenured partners?
Partners with under 60 days on the platform averaged 29.4 minutes per delivery, compared to 24.2 minutes for partners with over 180 days. Rookie drivers spend more time navigating unfamiliar residential layouts and traffic choke points.

### 9. What was the issue with the FLAT75 promo code?
Analyzing promo redemptions revealed that `FLAT75` was applied heavily on orders under Rs 250. Providing a Rs 75 discount on a Rs 200 order resulted in a negative contribution margin of -Rs 14 after accounting for restaurant take-rates and partner payouts. I recommended setting a minimum basket threshold of Rs 399 for `FLAT75`.

### 10. If you had more time or data, how would you extend this project?
I would integrate geospatial GPS route telemetry to separate time spent at traffic signals from restaurant pickup waiting time. Additionally, I would build an automated rider dispatch simulation in Python using SimPy to model the throughput impact of order batching during peak dinner hours.
