## Learning objectives

- Explain what a Data Analyst does, in two sentences, to a friend who is not technical.
- Walk through a realistic day of a junior analyst, hour by hour.
- Name eight job titles that all use analyst skills, and say how they differ.
- Choose which titles to search for on job portals.

## Why this matters for a Data Analyst job

You cannot prepare well for a job you do not understand. Many freshers think a Data Analyst "makes charts all day" or "does machine learning". Both ideas are wrong. If you know the real work, you will study the right skills in the right order, and you will answer "Why do you want this role?" with confidence.

<RealWorld>
In a fintech company in Bengaluru, the collections team asks every Monday: "Which loan accounts missed their EMI last week, and in which cities?" An analyst pulls the data with SQL, checks it, builds a small table and a chart, and sends it by 11 AM. The team then decides whom to call first. That is the job: turn a business question into a clear, trusted answer.
</RealWorld>

## Concept

### The one-line definition

A **Data Analyst** answers business questions using data. The path is always the same:

1. **Understand the question.** What does the manager really want to know?
2. **Get the data.** Usually from a database with SQL, or from Excel or CSV files.
3. **Clean the data.** Fix duplicates, blanks, wrong dates and spelling differences.
4. **Analyse.** Group, compare, calculate, find patterns.
5. **Present.** A table, a chart, a dashboard or a short written summary.
6. **Recommend.** "Because of this, we should do that."

Think of an analyst as a **translator**. The business speaks in questions ("Why are sales dropping in Pune?"). The data speaks in rows and columns. The analyst translates in both directions.

### A realistic day for a junior analyst

This is a fictional but typical day at a mid-size company. Your day will differ, but the shape is similar.

| Time | Activity | Tool |
|---|---|---|
| 9:30 | Check that the overnight dashboard refreshed. Fix it if a data load failed. | Power BI, SQL |
| 10:00 | Stand-up with the team. A manager asks: "Why did UPI failures rise on Saturday?" | Meeting |
| 10:30 | Write SQL to count failed payments by bank and hour for the last 14 days. | SQL |
| 12:00 | Notice 200 duplicate rows. Find the cause, remove them, note it in the document. | SQL, Excel |
| 13:00 | Lunch | |
| 14:00 | Build a small chart and a three-line summary of the findings. | Excel or Power BI |
| 15:30 | Send the summary. A manager replies: "Can you split this by city?" | Email, chat |
| 16:30 | Update the weekly MIS report that goes to senior leadership. | Excel |
| 17:30 | Write down what you did and learned. Plan tomorrow. | Notes |

Notice what is **not** in the list: no complex machine learning, no hours of pure chart design. Most time goes to understanding questions, cleaning data and communicating answers.

<Tip>
Your communication skill matters as much as your SQL. A correct answer that nobody understands has no value. Practise writing short, clear summaries from day one.
</Tip>

### Skills used daily

- **SQL**: the most important skill. Almost every analyst job needs it.
- **Excel**: for quick analysis, reports and sharing with non-technical people.
- **A BI tool** (Power BI or Tableau): for dashboards that refresh automatically.
- **Basic statistics**: averages, percentages, spotting outliers, understanding "is this change real or random?".
- **Python (pandas)**: useful for bigger files and repeated work. Often a plus for freshers, a must for some roles.
- **Business sense**: knowing what a "good" number looks like for the business.

### Job titles you should search for

The same skills appear under many names. Search all of them, because each company uses its own words.

| Title | What it usually means | Main tools | Fit for a fresher |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Data Analyst** | The general role: queries, analysis, dashboards, recommendations. | SQL, Excel, BI tool, sometimes Python | High, but competitive |
| **Junior Data Analyst** | Same as above with closer guidance from a senior. | SQL, Excel, BI | Very high |
| **MIS Executive** | MIS means Management Information System. You prepare daily and weekly reports, mostly in Excel. Often more routine work. | Excel, some SQL | Very high, a good first step |
| **Reporting Analyst** | You own a set of recurring reports and dashboards and keep them accurate. | SQL, Power BI, Excel | High |
| **Business Analyst** | Sits between business and tech. Gathers requirements, writes documents, uses data to support decisions. Varies a lot by company. | Excel, SQL, documentation, communication | Medium to high |
| **BI Analyst** | Focuses on dashboards and data models. | Power BI or Tableau, SQL, DAX | High if you know a BI tool well |
| **Operations Analyst** | Analyses processes: delivery times, support tickets, loan processing, fraud queues. | Excel, SQL | High, many openings |
| **Product Analyst** | Studies how users use an app: funnels, retention, experiments. | SQL, Python, statistics | Lower for freshers, fast-growing |

<Warning>
Read the job description, not only the title. Some "Data Analyst" posts are really data entry work, and some "Business Analyst" posts are heavy on documents with little data. Look for words like SQL, dashboard, analysis and insights.
</Warning>

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Turning a vague request into a clear task

A manager says: "Our app feels slow, can you check the data?"

A good analyst does not start with SQL. They ask questions first:

- Slow in what way: app opening, payment, or loading history?
- Since when? Which phones or cities?
- What number would tell us it is fixed?

After the answers, the vague request becomes: "Find the average payment time per day for the last 30 days, split by Android and iPhone, and flag days above 4 seconds."

Now the work is clear. This skill, **clarifying the question**, separates good analysts from average ones.

### Example 2: Which title fits whom

Three freshers, three good first targets:

- **Riya** is strong in Excel and neat with reports. She should target **MIS Executive** and **Reporting Analyst** and learn SQL on the side.
- **Arjun** has finished SQL and Power BI practice with two dashboards on GitHub. He should target **Data Analyst**, **BI Analyst** and **Junior Data Analyst**.
- **Neha** enjoys app usage data and statistics. She should add **Product Analyst** but also apply to Data Analyst roles, because fresher Product Analyst posts are fewer.

### Example 3: A ladder, not a single job

Many people start as an MIS Executive or Junior Analyst, move to Data Analyst within 1 to 2 years, then to Senior Analyst, Analytics Manager, or sideways to Analytics Engineer or Data Engineer. A first job is a starting point. Pick the one that gives you real SQL work and a good manager.

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistake>
Searching only for "Data Scientist" jobs. Those roles usually need a master's degree or experience. Search for analyst titles instead.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake>
Learning ten tools shallowly. Employers prefer someone who can do SQL and Excel well and one BI tool properly.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake>
Ignoring the business side. If you cannot say what a number means for the company, your analysis is only arithmetic.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake>
Rejecting MIS or reporting roles as "not real analytics". They are an honest way to get paid experience, and you can move up.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake>
Applying to every post with "data" in the title without reading the description.
</CommonMistake>

## Interview angle

<InterviewAlert>
"What does a Data Analyst do?" and "Why do you want this role?" are among the first questions in fresher interviews. A vague answer here creates a weak first impression.
</InterviewAlert>

<InterviewQ q="Describe what a Data Analyst does in your own words.">
A Data Analyst takes a business question, finds the right data, cleans it, analyses it, and explains the result in a form the business can act on. For example, in a lending company I might find which cities have the highest late EMI payments and suggest where to focus calls. I see the job as half technical work (SQL, Excel, dashboards) and half communication.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="Why Data Analyst and not Data Scientist or Software Developer?">
I enjoy finding answers in data and explaining them, and I like seeing how my work changes a decision quickly. Analyst work lets me use my coding basics with SQL and Python while staying close to business problems. I also see it as a strong base from which I can grow later into analytics engineering or data engineering.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="Tell me about a time you worked with data.">
Pick a project, even a practice one. Structure the answer as: the question, the data you used, one cleaning problem you found, the method, the result, and what you would do next. A short story with a number in it is better than a list of tools.
</InterviewQ>

## Key takeaways

- A Data Analyst turns business questions into clear, trusted answers using data.
- Most of the day goes to understanding questions, cleaning data and communicating, not to fancy modelling.
- SQL, Excel and one BI tool are the core toolkit for freshers.
- Search many titles: Data Analyst, Junior DA, MIS Executive, Reporting Analyst, Business Analyst, BI Analyst, Operations Analyst, Product Analyst.
- Read the job description, because titles are used loosely.
