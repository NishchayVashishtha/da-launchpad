## Why this matters for a Data Analyst job

<RealWorld>

Almost every analyst is asked "is this number normal?" or "is this result unusual?". The normal distribution and the z-score give you a shared language for those questions. You will also meet them in A/B tests, confidence intervals and outlier checks later in this module.

## Concept

### The bell curve

Imagine the heights of 10,000 college students in Pune. Most students are close to the average height. A few are very tall and a few are very short. If you draw the counts as bars, you get a hill shape that is symmetric around the middle. That hill is the **normal distribution**, also called the bell curve.

A normal curve has two numbers that set its full shape:

- The **mean** (written as the Greek letter mu) is the centre of the curve.
- The **standard deviation** (SD, written as sigma) is the typical distance of values from the mean. A small SD gives a tall, narrow bell. A large SD gives a wide, flat bell.

Many real measures are close to normal. Examples are heights, exam marks in a large class and the time a delivery takes on an ordinary day. Some measures are never normal, such as salaries and order values, because a few very large values pull the tail far to the right.

### The empirical rule (68-95-99.7)

For a normal curve, the share of values near the mean follows a fixed pattern. This is called the **empirical rule**:

| Distance from the mean | Share of values inside |
|---|---|
| within 1 SD | about 68% |
| within 2 SD | about 95% |
| within 3 SD | about 99.7% |

So if a process is normal, almost everything sits within 3 SD. A value beyond 3 SD is rare, about 3 in 1,000 in total across both tails.

<Tip>
Remember the rule as "68, 95, 99.7" and the three distances "1, 2, 3". You can then sketch the curve in your head in an interview.
</Tip>

### The z-score: how many SDs from the mean?

Raw numbers are hard to compare. A score of 78 in maths and a score of 65 in statistics may not mean the same thing, because each test has its own average and spread. The **z-score** fixes that. It says how many standard deviations a value sits above or below the mean.

<FormulaCard title="z-score">
z = (x - mu) / sigma

`x` is the value, `mu` is the mean and `sigma` is the standard deviation. A z of +2 means two SDs above the mean. A z of -1.5 means one and a half SDs below.
</FormulaCard>

A z-score puts every normal variable on the same **standard normal** scale, which has mean 0 and SD 1. Once standardised, you can compare a test with a delivery time or a sales figure.

<Warning>
The sign matters. A negative z is below the mean, and a z of 0 is exactly the mean. Do not treat -2 as "less unusual" than +2. Both are two SDs away.
</Warning>

### From z-score to percentile

A **percentile** says what share of values sit below a value. For a normal curve, you can read it from a table or calculate it with the error function `erf`, which is part of Python's `math` module (no SciPy needed).

| z-score | Share below (percentile) |
|---|---|
| 0 | 50.00% |
| 1 | 84.13% |
| 1.64 | 94.95% |
| 1.96 | 97.50% |
| 2.58 | 99.51% |

<FormulaCard title="Normal CDF with erf">
Percentile = 0.5 x (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2)))

In Python: `0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z / math.sqrt(2)))`. Multiply by 100 to get a percentage.
</FormulaCard>

A z of 1.96 leaves 2.5% in the upper tail. That is why 1.96 marks the edges of the central 95% of a normal curve.

### Is my data normal enough?

You should check before you trust a z-score or a percentile from a normal table. Three quick checks:

- **Mean close to median.** On a symmetric bell, the mean and median are about equal. A big gap means skew.
- **Histogram shape.** Look for one hump and a symmetric tail on both sides.
- **Empirical rule test.** Count the share within 1, 2 and 3 SDs. If you get about 68, 95 and 99.7, the normal model is a fair guess.

Delivery times are roughly normal but have a longer right tail. The normal model still gives a useful answer for the bulk of orders. Order values are much less normal, and the z-score percentile for them can mislead.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: How unusual is a 70-minute delivery?

We take the delivered orders from the `food_delivery` deliveries table. We remove duplicate rows by `delivery_id` first, because the same order should not count twice. Then we find the mean and SD of `actual_minutes`.

```python
import math
import pandas as pd

def load(dataset, table):
    return pd.read_csv(f"public/data/csv/{dataset}/{table}.csv")

d = load("food_delivery", "deliveries").drop_duplicates(subset=["delivery_id"])
a = d.loc[d.delivery_status == "Delivered", "actual_minutes"].dropna()

mu = a.mean()
sd = a.std()
print("rows:", len(a), "mean:", round(mu, 2), "sd:", round(sd, 2))

z70 = (70 - mu) / sd
pct70 = 0.5 * (1 + math.erf(z70 / math.sqrt(2))) * 100
print("z(70):", round(z70, 2), "percentile:", round(pct70, 1))
```

**Output:**

```text
rows: 14290 mean: 44.88 sd: 13.17
z(70): 1.91 percentile: 97.2
```

A 70-minute delivery sits 1.91 SDs above the average. About 97 percent of deliveries finish faster. So it is slow, but it is not a freak event.

### Example 2: Counting the extremes

How many orders are more than 2 SDs from the mean, in either direction? Under a perfect normal curve, about 4.6% would be (100 minus 95.4). Here is the real share.

```python
z = (a - mu) / sd
print("share with |z| > 2:", round((z.abs() > 2).mean() * 100, 2))
print("count:", int((z.abs() > 2).sum()))
```

**Output:**

```text
share with |z| > 2: 4.23
count: 605
```

The share is close to the normal value, so the rule is a reasonable model here. The counts also show that the right tail is a bit heavier, which matches the skew we saw earlier.

### Example 3: Comparing two different tests

Rahul scored 82 in a test with mean 70 and SD 8. Priya scored 74 in a different test with mean 60 and SD 6. Who did better compared with their own class?

| Student | Score | Mean | SD | z-score |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rahul | 82 | 70 | 8 | (82 - 70) / 8 = 1.5 |
| Priya | 74 | 60 | 6 | (74 - 60) / 6 = 2.33 |

Priya has the higher z-score, so she did better relative to her class. Her raw score is lower, but her test was harder for the class, so her result stands out more.

### Example 4: Checking the empirical rule on real data

We can test the 68-95-99.7 rule directly on the same deliveries.

```python
for k in [1, 2, 3]:
    print(f"share within {k} SD:", round((z.abs() <= k).mean() * 100, 2))
```

**Output:**

```text
share within 1 SD: 70.37
share within 2 SD: 95.77
share within 3 SD: 99.38
```

The results are close to 68, 95 and 99.7. The 1 SD share is a little higher than 68 because the data is slightly skewed to the right. So the rule is a good guide, not an exact law.

## Common mistakes

- **Using a z-score on very skewed data and trusting the percentile.** Money values and wait times with long tails can give a misleading percentile. Check the shape first.
- **Mixing up sample and population SD.** Pandas `std()` uses the sample formula by default. The difference is tiny for large data, but it matters for small groups.
- **Forgetting to subtract the mean.** `70 / 13.17` is not a z-score. Always compute `(x - mean) / SD`.
- **Reading z = 2 as "2% of the data".** A z of 2 is two SDs away. The share beyond it is about 2.3% in one tail and about 4.6% in both tails.
- **Using a one-tail table value for a two-tail question.** For the middle 95% of a normal curve, the cut-off is 1.96 on each side. For only the top 5%, the cut-off is 1.645.

## Interview angle

<InterviewAlert>
Interviewers often ask about normality because many models assume it. They want to see that you know the 68-95-99.7 rule by heart, can compute a z-score by hand and can say when the normal model is a poor fit.
</InterviewAlert>

<InterviewQ q="What is a z-score and why is it useful?">
A z-score is the number of standard deviations a value is from the mean: (value - mean) / SD. It puts values from different scales on one common scale, so I can compare a test score with a delivery time. It also turns any normal value into a percentile, which makes "how unusual is this?" easy to answer.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="Explain the 68-95-99.7 rule in simple words.">
For a normal distribution, about 68% of values are within 1 SD of the mean, about 95% are within 2 SD and about 99.7% are within 3 SD. It is a quick way to judge how rare a value is. For example, a value more than 3 SD away is very rare if the process is normal.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="How would you check quickly whether a column is roughly normal?">
I compare the mean and median, since they are close for a symmetric curve. I plot a histogram and look for one hump. Then I count the share within 1, 2 and 3 SDs and compare with 68, 95 and 99.7. Large skew or a heavy tail means the normal model is a poor fit.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="What is the difference between a z-score and a percentile?">
A z-score is a distance from the mean measured in SDs. A percentile is the share of values below a given value. For a normal curve, a z-score maps to one percentile, so I can move between them. Percentiles work for any distribution, but the z-to-percentile mapping only holds for normal data.
</InterviewQ>

## Key takeaways

- The normal curve is a symmetric bell set by its mean and standard deviation.
- The empirical rule says about 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values fall within 1, 2 and 3 SDs.
- A z-score, (x - mean) / SD, measures how many SDs a value is from the mean and puts different scales on one footing.
- Convert a z-score to a percentile with a table or `0.5 * (1 + erf(z / sqrt(2)))`.
- Check for skew before trusting a normal model. Money values and long-tail times often break it.
