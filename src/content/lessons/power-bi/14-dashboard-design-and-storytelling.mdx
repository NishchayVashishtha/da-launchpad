## Learning objectives

- Place visuals where readers look first.
- Design a KPI card that shows value, comparison and trend.
- Remove clutter and use colour sparingly.
- Write insight titles and a three-line summary.

## Why this matters for a Data Analyst job

<RealWorld>
Two analysts in a Hyderabad company build dashboards from the same data. One makes 14 charts in rainbow colours. The other makes one page with four numbers, one trend and a title: "Support attrition is 34%, double the company average". The second analyst gets promoted. The skill is not technical; it is clarity.
</RealWorld>

Hiring managers look at your portfolio screenshots for about 15 seconds. Design decides whether they keep looking.

## Concept

### Know the audience and the decision

Before you place a single visual, answer three questions:

1. **Who** will read this? (CEO, store manager, analyst)
2. **What decision** will they make? (cut influencer budget, hire more support staff)
3. **How often** will they look? (daily glance or monthly deep dive)

An executive dashboard is short: 4 to 6 numbers and 2 to 3 charts. An analyst page can be denser, with filters and tables.

### Layout and the Z-pattern

People in left-to-right languages scan a page in a **Z shape**: top-left, across to top-right, diagonally down to bottom-left, then across to bottom-right.

<Mermaid chart={`flowchart LR

  B --> C["3 Middle: main trend or chart"]
  C --> D["4 Bottom: detail and ranking"]`} caption="Place the most important item where the eye lands first and move from summary to detail." />

Practical layout:

- Top strip: title, one-line subtitle, date filter.
- Row 1: 4 to 6 KPI cards.
- Row 2: the main chart (usually a trend) taking the largest area.
- Row 3: supporting charts (breakdown, ranking) and a table if needed.
- Left or top strip: slicers, always in the same place on every page.
- Leave white space. It is not wasted; it groups things.

### KPI cards

A good KPI card is more than a number. It answers: how much, compared to what, and which way is it going.

| Card element | Example |
|---|---|
| Label | Net Sales |
| Value | ₹3.00 crore |
| Comparison | +19.7% vs last year |
| Trend | tiny sparkline |
| Colour | green if good, red if bad, grey for neutral |

Use the **Card (new)** visual or the **KPI** visual, which has a target and trend axis. Format large numbers in lakh or crore when your audience is Indian, using custom formats or DAX `FORMAT`.

### Colour with meaning

- Use **grey** as the base and **one accent** for what matters.
- Use **red/green** only for good/bad, and add a symbol or label for colour-blind readers.
- Use sequential colour (light to dark of one hue) for magnitude.
- Keep a **consistent meaning**: if red means "bad" on page 1, it must not mean "Region North" on page 2.
- Check contrast: dark text on light background, minimum font size 10 to 12 pt.

### Declutter

Remove everything that does not help the reader:

- gridlines and heavy borders,
- redundant axis titles when the chart title says it,
- legends if you can label lines directly,
- decimals nobody needs (₹30.03 crore not ₹300,277,320.00),
- 3D effects, shadows, background images.

Ask of each element: "If I remove this, does the reader lose anything?" If not, delete it.

### Titles that state the insight

| Weak title | Strong title |
|---|---|
| Sales by Region | West brings in 41% of sales; East only 11% |
| Attrition by Department | Customer Support and Sales lose about 1 in 3 employees |
| Failure Rate by Bank | Kaveri Co-op Bank fails 6% of UPI payments, more than twice most banks |
| Monthly Sales | Sales peak every Oct and Nov and grew 19.7% in 2024 |

Formula: **subject + number + comparison (+ so what)**. Use Power BI's dynamic titles (conditional formatting with a DAX measure) if the number changes with filters.

### Data storytelling

A dashboard becomes a story when you structure it like one:

1. **Context** (what is happening): the headline KPIs.
2. **Conflict** (what is the problem): the chart that shows the gap.
3. **Cause** (why): the driver breakdown.
4. **Action** (what to do): a short recommendation box or text.

Write an executive summary of three lines: **What**, **So what**, **Now what**. Place it on a "Summary" page or as a text box at the top.

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Rebuilding a messy retail page

Before: 12 visuals, rainbow colours, title "Dashboard". After:

1. Title: "Retail 2023 to 2024: sales up 19.7%, four stores held back by stockouts".
2. Row of 4 cards: Net Sales, YoY %, Target attainment, Stockout rate.
3. One line chart of monthly Net Sales, 2023 vs 2024.
4. One bar of net sales by store with the 4 stockout stores in red.
5. Slicers: Year, Region at the left.

Visuals dropped from 12 to 7 and every one answers a question.

### Example 2: Writing an insight title for HR

Chart: attrition rate by department. Draft: "Attrition by Department". Better: "Support and Sales lose about 1 in 3 employees; Finance loses 1 in 7". Check the numbers against the data: Customer Support 33.6%, Sales 31.2%, Finance 14.6%. The title is truthful and useful.

### Example 3: A KPI card with context in DAX

```dax
YoY Label =
VAR g = [YoY %]
RETURN IF(g >= 0, "▲ ", "▼ ") & FORMAT(ABS(g), "0.0%") & " vs last year"
```

Use this measure in the card's "reference label" so the card says "▲ 19.7% vs last year".

### Example 4: A three-line summary for fintech

- **What:** UPI failure rate is 2.7%, but Kaveri Co-op Bank is at 6.1%.
- **So what:** Its failures are concentrated at peak hours and are mostly server timeouts, so customers there face repeated failures when they need payments most.
- **Now what:** Escalate to the bank, show customers a retry message, and track its failure rate weekly.

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistake>
Starting with visuals instead of the decision. Without a decision in mind you will fill the page with whatever is easy to chart.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake>
Using red and green only. About 1 in 12 men are colour-blind. Add arrows, labels or patterns.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake>
Showing too many digits. ₹3,00,27,732 is harder to read than ₹3.0 crore. Round for humans.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake>
Descriptive titles such as "Chart 1". A title should say what to notice.
</CommonMistake>

<CommonMistake>
Changing the position of slicers and colours between pages. Consistency lets readers trust the layout.
</CommonMistake>

## Interview angle

<InterviewAlert>
A typical question: "Walk me through a dashboard you built. Why did you design it that way?" Talk about the audience, the decision, and what you removed.
</InterviewAlert>

<InterviewQ q="What are your principles for designing a dashboard?">
I start with the audience and the decision. I put the main KPIs at the top-left following the Z-pattern, one main chart in the middle, and details at the bottom. I use grey with one accent colour, remove clutter, label with insight titles, and keep the page to 5 to 8 visuals.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="How do you write a good chart title?">
I state the finding: subject, number and comparison. For example "Kaveri Co-op Bank fails 6% of UPI payments, more than twice most banks" instead of "Failure rate by bank".
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="How do you present a dashboard to a non-technical executive?">
I start with the headline and recommendation, then show the one or two charts that prove it, and keep technical details for questions. I use plain words and rupees or percentages, not jargon.
</InterviewQ>

## Key takeaways

- Start with the audience and decision, not the charts.
- Use the Z-pattern: summary at the top, detail below.
- A KPI card needs value, comparison and trend.
- Use grey plus one accent colour and remove clutter.
- Titles should state the insight; end with a recommendation.
