## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Define customer churn and revenue churn, avoiding common denominator errors.
- Query transactional subscription databases in SQL to calculate churn rates across billing cycles and plan tiers.
- Separate voluntary churn (user-initiated cancellations) from involuntary churn (failed recurring card/UPI auto-debits).
- Measure subscriber tenure at the time of cancellation to evaluate onboarding friction.
- Convert monthly churn rates into annualized attrition figures using compound survival mathematics.

## Why this matters for a Data Analyst job

<RealWorld>
The leadership team at a SaaS streaming app was celebrating 10,000 new subscribers acquired in Q1. But during the quarterly finance review, the CFO delivered bad news: Net Recurring Revenue was shrinking. The company had an 8% monthly churn rate. 

At 8% monthly churn, more than 63% of the customer base vanishes every twelve months. The marketing team was pouring expensive ad spend into a leaky bucket, constantly acquiring new customers just to replace the ones quietly cancelling through the back door.
</RealWorld>

Reducing churn is the highest-leverage growth strategy in recurring revenue businesses. Acquiring a new customer costs 5 to 7 times more than retaining an existing one. As a data analyst, you will be called upon to diagnose: *"Why are subscribers leaving?"*, *"Is churn concentrated in monthly or annual plans?"*, and *"How much recurring revenue are we losing each month?"*

## Concept

### Customer Churn vs Revenue Churn

In subscription businesses, churn is measured across two distinct dimensions:

1. **Customer (Logo) Churn Rate**:
   The percentage of paying customers who terminate their subscription over a defined period:
   `Customer Churn Rate = (Customers Lost in Period / Customers at Start of Period) * 100`

2. **Revenue Churn Rate**:
   The percentage of recurring revenue lost to cancellations and plan downgrades:
   `Gross Revenue Churn = (Churned MRR + Contraction MRR) / Starting MRR * 100`

<Tip>
**Revenue Churn vs Logo Churn:** If you have 100 customers paying Rs 100/month and 1 enterprise customer paying Rs 10,000/month, losing 5 small customers is a 5% customer churn but only a 2.5% revenue churn. Conversely, losing the single enterprise account is a 1% customer churn but a massive 50% revenue churn. Always track both.
</Tip>

### Voluntary vs Involuntary Churn

Cancellations fall into two fundamentally different operational buckets:

- **Voluntary Churn**: The user actively visits their account settings and clicks "Cancel Subscription." Root causes include perceived lack of value, high pricing, poor user experience, or changing customer needs. Fixes require product enhancements, onboarding tutorials, and win-back offers.
- **Involuntary (Passive) Churn**: The customer intends to remain subscribed, but their recurring payment fails due to an expired debit/credit card, insufficient funds, or a banking auto-debit switch decline (`payment_failed` event). Fixes require dunning logic: automated email/SMS payment reminders, intelligent retry schedules, and secondary UPI mandate fallbacks.

<FormulaCard title="Annualizing Monthly Churn">
Because customers leave sequentially month after month, monthly churn compounds exponentially. The annual retention rate is `(1 - Monthly Churn)^12`:
`Annualized Churn Rate = 100% - (1 - Monthly Churn Rate)^12`

For example, a monthly churn rate of 5.0% does not equal 60% annual churn (linear math); it equals:
`100% - (1 - 0.05)^12 = 100% - (0.95)^12 = 100% - 54.04% = 45.96% ≈ 46.0%`
</FormulaCard>

## Worked examples

All examples in this section query `public/data/sqlite/subscription.sqlite` using SQLite.

### Example 1: Overall Paid Customer Churn
We audit distinct paid subscriptions (`paid_start_date IS NOT NULL`) and count how many have cancelled (`status = 'cancelled'`):

```sql
WITH s AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT * FROM subscriptions
)
SELECT
  COUNT(*) AS paid,
  SUM(CASE WHEN status = 'cancelled' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS cancelled,
  ROUND(AVG(CASE WHEN status = 'cancelled' THEN 100.0 ELSE 0.0 END), 1) AS churn_pct
FROM s
WHERE paid_start_date IS NOT NULL;
```

**Real Output from subscription.sqlite:**
| paid | cancelled | churn_pct |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1193 | 654 | 54.8 |

Across the 2-year lifecycle of the platform, 654 out of 1,193 paid subscribers have cancelled, yielding a cumulative lifetime churn rate of 54.8%.

### Example 2: Churn by Billing Frequency
Comparing churn between monthly and annual billing cycles:

```sql
WITH s AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT * FROM subscriptions
)
SELECT
  billing_cycle,
  COUNT(*) AS paid,
  SUM(CASE WHEN status = 'cancelled' THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS cancelled,
  ROUND(AVG(CASE WHEN status = 'cancelled' THEN 100.0 ELSE 0.0 END), 1) AS churn_pct
FROM s
WHERE paid_start_date IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY billing_cycle
ORDER BY churn_pct DESC;
```

**Real Output from subscription.sqlite:**
| billing_cycle | paid | cancelled | churn_pct |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| monthly | 998 | 621 | 62.2 |
| annual | 195 | 33 | 16.9 |

**Key Insight:** Monthly subscribers churn at nearly $4\times$ the rate of annual subscribers (62.2% vs 16.9%). Annual subscribers make a long-term upfront commitment, bypassing monthly renewal friction.

### Example 3: Monthly Revenue Lost by Tier
Evaluating recurring monthly revenue lost to cancellations across pricing plans:

```sql
WITH s AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT * FROM subscriptions
)
SELECT
  plan,
  COUNT(*) AS cancelled_subs,
  SUM(price_inr) AS lost_mrr
FROM s
WHERE paid_start_date IS NOT NULL AND billing_cycle = 'monthly' AND status = 'cancelled'
GROUP BY plan
ORDER BY lost_mrr DESC;
```

**Real Output from subscription.sqlite:**
| plan | cancelled_subs | lost_mrr |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Standard | 319 | 95381 |
| Premium | 99 | 49401 |
| Basic | 203 | 30247 |

Standard plan cancellations represent the largest financial leak (Rs 95,381 per month), accounting for more lost revenue than Basic and Premium combined.

### Example 4: Subscriber Tenure at Cancellation
We group cancelled paid subscribers into tenure intervals to identify when users abandon the service:

```sql
WITH s AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT * FROM subscriptions
  WHERE paid_start_date IS NOT NULL AND status = 'cancelled' AND cancel_date IS NOT NULL
),
b AS (
  SELECT
    CASE
      WHEN julianday(cancel_date) - julianday(paid_start_date) <= 30 THEN '0-30'
      WHEN julianday(cancel_date) - julianday(paid_start_date) <= 60 THEN '31-60'
      WHEN julianday(cancel_date) - julianday(paid_start_date) <= 90 THEN '61-90'
      ELSE '90+'
    END AS tenure_bucket
  FROM s
)
SELECT
  tenure_bucket,
  COUNT(*) AS subs,
  ROUND(COUNT(*) * 100.0 / (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM b), 1) AS share_pct
FROM b
GROUP BY tenure_bucket
ORDER BY
  CASE tenure_bucket
    WHEN '0-30' THEN 1
    WHEN '31-60' THEN 2
    WHEN '61-90' THEN 3
    ELSE 4
  END;
```

**Real Output from subscription.sqlite:**
| tenure_bucket | subs | share_pct |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 0-30 | 208 | 31.8 |
| 31-60 | 78 | 11.9 |
| 61-90 | 56 | 8.6 |
| 90+ | 312 | 47.7 |

Nearly one-third of all churned subscribers (31.8%, 208 users) cancel within their very first month of paying. This early cliff points to buyer remorse or onboarding disappointment immediately after trial conversion.

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistake>
- **Counting trial expirations as churn**: Users whose free trials expired without entering a credit card or making a payment are unconverted leads, not churned subscribers.
- **Using the ending customer count in the denominator**: Calculating churn as `Cancellations / Ending Customers` understates attrition when the business is acquiring new users.
- **Conflating monthly and annual plans**: Blending annual subscribers (who cannot churn until Month 12) with monthly subscribers produces an artificially depressed blended monthly churn figure.
- **Linear annualization**: Multiplying 5% monthly churn by 12 to claim 60% annual churn, ignoring compound survival probability (actual is 46.0%).
- **Offering blanket discounts for involuntary churn**: Offering a 20% renewal discount to a user whose card simply expired wastes margin without resolving the payment gateway error.
</CommonMistake>

## Interview angle

<InterviewAlert>
A classic analytics interview scenario: "Our customer churn rate increased from 4% to 6% last month. Walk me through your diagnostic game plan."
</InterviewAlert>

<InterviewQ q="Customer churn rose from 4% to 6% last month. How do you investigate?">
I investigate churn spikes using a structured 4-step diagnostic protocol:
1. **Voluntary vs Involuntary Decomposition**: Check the payment event logs. Did `payment_failed` events surge due to a banking auto-debit switch glitch, or did manual cancellations increase?
2. **Cohort & Vintage Analysis**: Check whether the churn is coming from older, established subscribers (indicating a product or competitive issue) or from recently acquired cohorts (indicating a poor acquisition channel or misleading ad campaign).
3. **Segmentation by Plan & Billing Cycle**: Isolate whether the spike is concentrated in Basic Monthly plans, or if high-value Annual/Premium accounts are cancelling.
4. **Usage Telemetry Preceding Cancellation**: Analyze feature engagement and app opens in the 30 days prior to churn. If cancellations occurred with high prior usage, it indicates pricing dissatisfaction; if usage was zero, it reflects disengagement.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="What is Net Revenue Retention (NRR), and why is it possible for NRR to exceed 100%?">
Net Revenue Retention (NRR) measures the percentage of recurring revenue retained from existing customers over a period, incorporating churn, downgrades (contractions), and upgrades (expansions):
`NRR = (Starting MRR - Churned MRR - Contraction MRR + Expansion MRR) / Starting MRR * 100`

NRR can exceed 100% when **Expansion MRR** (existing customers upgrading to higher tiers or buying more seats/add-ons) surpasses the revenue lost to churn and contractions. When NRR is greater than 100%, the business grows organically even if marketing acquires zero new customers.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="Why do annual subscription plans consistently show lower churn than monthly plans?">
Annual plans display lower churn due to three fundamental factors:
1. **Self-Selection**: Customers willing to pay 12 months upfront have higher intent, greater budget, and higher expected utility from the product.
2. **Reduced Friction Points**: A monthly subscriber evaluates whether to renew 12 times a year, exposing the account to 12 potential payment failures and cancellation decisions; an annual subscriber evaluates renewal only once a year.
3. **Onboarding Runway**: Annual subscribers have ample time to integrate the product into their workflows, overcoming initial learning curves before facing a renewal decision.
</InterviewQ>

## Key takeaways

- Churn measures customer and revenue attrition; evaluate churn strictly against the starting customer denominator.
- In our subscription data, 31.8% of all cancellations occur within the first 30 days of paying, pointing to onboarding friction.
- Monthly subscribers churn at nearly $4\times$ the rate of annual subscribers (62.2% vs 16.9%); pushing annual plans is a powerful retention lever.
- Separate voluntary cancellations from involuntary payment declines; never waste pricing discounts on users whose cards merely expired.
- Monthly churn compounds exponentially: a 5% monthly churn rate equals a 46% annual customer turnover.
