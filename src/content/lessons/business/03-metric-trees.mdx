## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson, you will be able to:
- Build complete metric trees for e-commerce revenue, UPI payment volume, SaaS subscription recurring revenue, and healthcare operations.
- Separate multiplicative driver trees (Rate $\times$ Volume) from additive segment trees (summing channels or categories).
- Apply the MECE framework to ensure driver trees cover 100% of the metric without gaps or overlapping components.
- Trace top-line variance down to specific operational leaves to explain why a primary business metric shifted.
- Map terminal leaves to functional organizational owners (Marketing, Product, Engineering, Operations).

## Why this matters for a Data Analyst job

<RealWorld>
Imagine walking into a Monday morning business review where the Head of Product announces: *"Our marketplace revenue dropped 20% in December. Marketing says traffic is fine, Engineering says the servers were fast, and Merchandising says prices didn't change. Nishchay, what happened?"*

Without a structured metric tree, analysts jump straight into writing random SQL queries, guessing causes, or drowning stakeholders in twenty uncoordinated charts. With a metric tree, you immediately decompose Revenue into Orders $\times$ Average Order Value (AOV), split Orders into Transacting Buyers $\times$ Frequency, and show in under two minutes that while frequency held steady, unique active buyers contracted 12.5% because post-Diwali re-engagement campaigns stalled.
</RealWorld>

In analytics interviews, metric decomposition questions are universal: *"How would you break down Uber's revenue?"*, *"How does Swiggy analyze food delivery GMV?"*, or *"Why did clinic profitability fall?"* Building structured metric trees demonstrates mature, structured business thinking.

## Concept

### What is a Metric Tree?

A **metric tree** is a hierarchical visual framework that places a primary organizational goal at the root (the top) and recursively decomposes it into contributing mathematical drivers (the branches and leaves) until each terminal leaf represents an operational lever that a single team can influence.

<Tip>
**The Electricity Bill Analogy:** If your monthly electricity bill surges from Rs 2,000 to Rs 3,500, you don't panic or guess. You break the bill into its multiplicative drivers: `Bill = Units Consumed (kWh) * Tariff per Unit`. If the tariff is unchanged, you decompose Units Consumed additively across appliances: Air Conditioner + Refrigerator + Water Heater. You quickly discover that the AC ran 12 hours a day during a heatwave. That is a metric tree in daily life.
</Tip>

### Multiplicative vs Additive Decompositions

Metric trees split numbers using two mathematical structures:

1. **Multiplicative Decompositions (Product of Drivers)**:
   Used when top-line volume is the mathematical product of independent conversion rates and ticket sizes.
   - `E-Commerce Revenue = Unique Visitors * Conversion Rate * AOV`
   - `UPI TPV = Active Users * (Transactions / Active User) * Average Ticket Size * Success Rate`

2. **Additive Decompositions (Sum of Parts)**:
   Used when total volume is the sum of mutually exclusive segments or lifecycle cohorts.
   - `Total Orders = App Orders + Website Orders + Mobile Web Orders`
   - `Ending MRR = Starting MRR + New MRR + Expansion MRR - Churned MRR`

### The MECE Principle

Every sound metric tree must adhere to the **MECE** principle:
- **Mutually Exclusive**: Branches at the same tier must not overlap or double count (e.g., do not split orders into "Credit Card Orders" and "Android Orders" at the same level).
- **Collectively Exhaustive**: The sum or product of the branches must equal 100% of the parent metric without missing unaccounted residuals.

<FormulaCard title="Percentage Change of Multiplicative Products">
When a metric is the product of two factors (`Y = A * B`), the exact percentage change in `Y` is:
`Change % in Y = (1 + Change % in A) * (1 + Change % in B) - 1`

For example, if orders fall by `-14.72%` and AOV falls by `-6.81%`:
`Revenue Change = (1 - 0.1472) * (1 - 0.0681) - 1 = (0.8528 * 0.9319) - 1 = -20.53%`

Notice that simply adding percentages (`-14.72% + -6.81% = -21.53%`) is only a rough approximation; always compute the exact compound change.
</FormulaCard>

## Worked examples

### Example 1: Decomposing DesiCart's December Revenue Drop
Querying the `orders` table in `public/data/sqlite/ecommerce.sqlite` for delivered orders across November and December 2024:

```sql
WITH o AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT * FROM orders
  WHERE total_amount < 100000 AND order_status = 'Delivered'
)
SELECT
  strftime('%Y-%m', order_date) AS month,
  COUNT(*) AS delivered_orders,
  CAST(ROUND(SUM(total_amount)) AS INTEGER) AS revenue,
  ROUND(AVG(total_amount), 2) AS aov
FROM o
WHERE strftime('%Y-%m', order_date) IN ('2024-11', '2024-12')
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month ASC;
```

**Real Output from ecommerce.sqlite:**
| month | delivered_orders | revenue | aov |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2024-11 | 496 | 2591559 | 5224.92 |
| 2024-12 | 423 | 2059545 | 4868.90 |

**Driver Decomposition:**
1. **Delivered Orders**: fell from 496 to 423 ($\Delta = -14.72\%$).
2. **Average Order Value (AOV)**: fell from Rs 5,224.92 to Rs 4,868.90 ($\Delta = -6.81\%$).
3. **Realized Revenue**: fell from Rs 2,591,559 to Rs 2,059,545 ($\Delta = -20.53\%$).

Order volume contraction accounted for approximately 70% of the revenue drop, while lower basket size explained the remaining 30%.

### Example 2: Inspecting Customer Breadth vs Frequency
To find why delivered orders fell 14.72%, we step one level deeper into the tree: `Orders = Unique Buyers * Orders per Buyer`.

```sql
WITH o AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT * FROM orders
  WHERE total_amount < 100000
)
SELECT
  strftime('%Y-%m', order_date) AS month,
  COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS buyers,
  ROUND(COUNT(*) * 1.0 / COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id), 3) AS orders_per_buyer
FROM o
WHERE strftime('%Y-%m', order_date) IN ('2024-11', '2024-12')
GROUP BY month
ORDER BY month ASC;
```

**Real Output from ecommerce.sqlite:**
| month | buyers | orders_per_buyer |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 2024-11 | 448 | 1.190 |
| 2024-12 | 392 | 1.168 |

Active buyers fell from 448 to 392 ($-12.50\%$), whereas ordering frequency per buyer barely budged from 1.190 to 1.168 ($-1.85\%$). 

**The Diagnostic Finding:** The revenue drop was not caused by loyal customers buying less frequently; it was caused by a broad drop in transacting shoppers following the Diwali festive rush.

## Common mistakes

<CommonMistake>
- **Overlapping branches (Violating MECE)**: Splitting revenue into "Android Sales" and "Fashion Category Sales" at the same tier, creating double counting.
- **Stopping at vague non-actionable leaves**: Ending a tree at leaves like "Customer Satisfaction" or "Brand Perception" instead of measurable operational events like "P90 Page Load Latency" or "Coupon Redemption Rate".
- **Simply adding percentage changes**: Stating that $-14.7\%$ and $-6.8\%$ equals $-21.5\%$. Multiplicative variables compound.
- **Mixing time horizons**: Comparing quarterly churn against monthly acquisition cohorts within the same mathematical tree.
- **Building trees with unmeasurable data**: Adding branches that no internal logging pipeline tracks. Every leaf must correspond to an actual queryable table column.
</CommonMistake>

## Interview angle

<InterviewAlert>
In product and business analytics interviews, you will often be given a whiteboard prompt: "Decompose revenue for a food delivery platform like Zomato or Swiggy." Practice drawing the full tree in under 60 seconds while verbalizing the operational owner for each leaf.
</InterviewAlert>

<InterviewQ q="Break down food delivery marketplace revenue into a metric tree on a whiteboard.">
I would decompose food delivery net revenue into a multiplicative and additive structure:
- `Net Revenue = Commission Revenue + Customer Delivery Fees + Restaurant Ad Revenue`

Decomposing the primary driver (Commission Revenue):
- `Commission Revenue = Delivered Orders * AOV * Take Rate %`

Stepping into Delivered Orders:
- `Delivered Orders = Daily Active Users (DAU) * (Orders Placed / DAU) * (1 - Cancellation Rate %)`

And stepping into AOV:
- `AOV = Items per Order * Average Item Price`

Each leaf has a direct team owner:
- DAU → Marketing & CRM
- Orders/DAU & Cancellation Rate → Product UX & Fleet Operations
- Take Rate & Ad Revenue → Restaurant Partnerships / Monetization team.
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="How do you prioritize which branch of a metric tree to investigate when a metric moves?">
I prioritize branches using a 3-step filter:
1. **Mathematical Impact Sizing**: Calculate the contribution to total variance (`Variance Contribution = Delta Branch / Total Delta`). If 80% of the drop comes from Order Volume rather than AOV, I deprioritize AOV immediately.
2. **Segment Concentration**: Check whether the move is isolated to one segment (e.g. Android only, Kaveri Co-op Bank only, or Delhi NCR only) versus systemic across all categories.
3. **Actionability**: Focus resources on leaves the business can control (such as discount policies or app checkout crashes) rather than macro factors (like unseasonal monsoon rains).
</InterviewQ>

<InterviewQ q="What is the danger of optimizing an individual leaf in a metric tree without monitoring the root?">
Sub-optimizing a single leaf frequently triggers unintended trade-offs that damage the overall tree:
- If Marketing optimizes the **Visitor Traffic** leaf by buying cheap social media clicks, conversion rate collapses and blended CAC explodes.
- If Merchandising optimizes the **AOV** leaf by raising product prices or minimum order values, checkout conversion drops so sharply that total revenue contracts.
- If Ops cuts the **Delivery Partner Wage** leaf to reduce costs, partner churn rises, delivery latencies spike, and order cancellations surge.
</InterviewQ>

## Key takeaways

- A metric tree systematically breaks an aggregate number into actionable mathematical levers until each leaf has a functional owner.
- Multiplicative trees decompose volume and rates (Visitors $\times$ CVR $\times$ AOV); additive trees sum mutually exclusive segments or lifecycle cohorts.
- Follow the MECE rule: branches must never overlap (double count) and must collectively explain 100% of the parent metric.
- For small multiplicative shifts, the sum of percentage changes is an approximation; always compute the exact compound factor.
- Terminal leaves must be actionable operational levers (such as page load latency, stockout days, or bank timeout rates) rather than vague conceptual labels.
